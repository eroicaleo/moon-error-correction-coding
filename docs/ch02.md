## 2.2 Groups

### 2.2.4 Lagrange’s Theorem

#### Lemma 2.26

Every coset of $H$ in a group $G$ has the same number of elements.

**Proof**:

Let the coset be $a * H$. Then we can make an one-to-one mapping between
$a * H$ and $H$:

$$ 
f : a * H \longrightarrow H, a * h \longrightarrow h
$$

So $a * H$ has the same number of elements as $H$.

$\square$

#### Lemma 2.28

The distinct cosets of $H$ in a group $G$ are disjoint.

**Proof**:

Assume $a * H$ and $b * H$ are 2 distinct cosets of $H$, assume
$c \in a * H \cap b * H$, then we can find $h_1, h_2$ such that

$a h_1 = c = b h_2$, so we have $a h_1 h_2^{-1} = b$, so $b \in a * H$.
That means $a * H = b * H$.

Then we reach a contradiction.

$\square$

#### Theorem 2.29 (Lagrange’s theorem)

Let $G$ be a group of finite order and let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$.
Then the order of $H$ divides the order of $G$. That is, $|H|$ divides $|G|$.

**Proof**:

Just apply Lemma 2.26 and Lemma 2.28.

$\square$

### 2.2.5 Induced Operations; Isomorphism

#### Definition 2.36

Let $⟨G,*⟩$ be a group, $H$ a subgroup and let
$S = \{H_0 = H,H_1,H_2, ...,H_M \}$ be the set
of cosets of $H$ in $G$.
Then the induced operation between cosets $A$ and $B$ in $S$ is defined by

$$ 
A * B = C \text{ if and only if } a * b= c
$$

for any $a ∈ A, b ∈ B$ and some $c ∈ C$,
provided that this operation is well defined. The operation
is well defined if for every $a ∈ A$ and $b ∈ B, a * b ∈ C$;
there is thus no ambiguity in the induced operation.

#### Theorem 2.38

If $H$ is a subgroup of a commutative group $⟨G,*⟩$, the induced operation $*$
on the set of cosets of $H$ satisfies

$$ 
(a * b) * H = (a * H) * (b * H).
$$

Furthermore, the operation is well defined.

**Proof**:

We will prove it's well defined, Let
$a_1 \in a * H, b_1 \in b * H$, we want to show

$$ 
(a * b) * H = (a_1 * b_1) * H
$$

Since $a_1 \in a * H$, we have $a_1 = a h_1, h_1 \in H$.
Similarly, we have $b_1 = b h_2, h_2 \in H$.

Since $G$ is commutative, $a_1 b_1 = a h_1 b h_2 = (ab) (h_1h_2)$.
Since $H$ is a subgroup, $h_1 h_2 \in H$.

So we have

$$ 
(a * b) * H = (a_1 * b_1) * H
$$

Then the operation is well defined.

$\square$

## 2.4 Review of Linear Algebra

#### Definition 2.62

Let $W$ be a $k$-dimensional subspace of a vector space $V$. The set of all 
vectors $𝐮 ∈ V$
which are orthogonal to all the vectors of $W$ is called the dual space of
$W$ (sometimes called the orthogonal complement of $W$ or dual space), denoted 
$W_⟂$. (The symbol $W_⟂$ is sometimes pronounced $“W$ perp,” for “perpendicular.”) 
That is,

$$ 
W_⟂ = \{𝐮 ∈ V ∶ 𝐮 \cdot 𝐰 = 0 \text{ for all } 𝐰 ∈ W\}.
$$

$\square$

#### Theorem 2.64

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space of $n$-tuples,
$\mathbb{F}^n$, with a subspace $W$ of
dimension $k$. Let $U= W_⟂$ be the dual space of $W$. Then

$$ 
\dim(W_⟂) = \dim(V)−\dim(W) = n−k.
$$

**Proof**:

We will use a different approach compare to the textbook.

Let $e_1, \cdots, e_n$ be the standard basis of $\mathbb{F}^n$.
And $\psi_1, \cdots, \psi_n$ be the dual basis of $e_1, \cdots, e_n$.

Then we can construct an isomorphism from

$$ 
T: \mathbb{F}^n \longrightarrow (\mathbb{F}^{n})',
(a_1, \cdots, a_n) \longrightarrow
a_1 \psi_1 + \cdots + a_n \psi_n
$$

Then in fact, $W_⟂$ is isomorphic to the annihilator of $W$,
i.e. $W^0$. From Linear Algebra Done Right 4th edition,
3.125 dimension of the annihilator, we know

$$ 
\dim W^0 = \dim 𝑉 − \dim W.
$$

So 

$$ 
\dim(W_⟂) = \dim W^0 = \dim 𝑉 − \dim W
$$

$\square$
